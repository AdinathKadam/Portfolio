Case 01

A finance pipeline that returns the same number twice

In production use

Replacing a hand-assembled month-end with a governed pipeline — without taking Excel away from the analysts who live in it.

The problem

FP&A reporting fails in a recognisable way. Figures are exported by hand, stitched together in workbooks that quietly diverge, and reconciled under time pressure at close. The output looks finished, but nobody can walk a number back to its source without asking the one person who built the file.

Three things break at once: reproducibility — the same question asked twice gets two answers; continuity — the process lives in one person's head; and auditability — when leadership asks why a figure moved, the answer takes days.

The constraint that shaped the design

The obvious fix — move finance onto a platform and retrain everyone — is the one that usually fails. Analysts are fluent in Excel, they are fast in it, and the model they interrogate at 6pm on close day is the one they trust. So Excel stays. The fragility moves upstream, out of the workbook and into infrastructure.

The architecture

Six stages, each with one job and one owner — shown above. The full stage-by-stage specification is published in the Zenodo preprint; the argument for the pattern is in the SSRN working paper.

The decisions that matter

  • A cloud database, not a shared drive. Once landed data has one address, every downstream artefact can point at it instead of at a copy. Copies are how two workbooks start disagreeing.
  • Transformation logic in Power Query, not in cells. Steps are named, ordered and reviewable. A formula buried in row 4,812 is not.
  • Measures defined once, in the model. If gross margin is re-derived in each workbook, there is no such thing as gross margin — there are as many as there are analysts.
  • Reconciliation before anything downstream. Loaded totals are checked against source totals at the boundary, so a bad extract fails at ingestion rather than in a board pack.
  • No hand-keyed inputs past the source stage. The moment a number is typed in, its lineage ends.

What changes

Close stops depending on one person's memory. A figure can be walked back through the model, the transformation steps and the load to the extract it came from. The same question asked in March and in June returns the same number, and when it doesn't, the difference is explainable.

What the pattern costs

It moves work rather than removing it. Somebody has to own the transformation layer and keep it documented, and the semantic model has to be governed or the workbooks re-fragment within two quarters. The pattern buys reproducibility; it does not buy the absence of maintenance, and pretending otherwise is how these projects get abandoned.

Case 02

An FP&A agent that is not allowed to do arithmetic

Live prototype

Variance analysis, KPI commentary and premium forecasting for insurance finance — with the calculation layer and the language layer kept strictly apart.

The problem

The slowest part of close is rarely the numbers. It is the narrative — writing up what moved, by how much, and why, in language a management committee can act on. It is repetitive, it is late in the cycle, and it is the first thing to get thin when the calendar tightens.

The obvious application of a language model here is also the dangerous one. A model asked to both compute and explain will do neither reliably, and a plausible-sounding wrong figure in a board pack is worse than no commentary at all.

The design rule

The model never computes. Every figure in the output is produced deterministically — in SQL and Python, against the governed dataset — before the language layer is invoked. The model is handed those figures and asked only to narrate them. If a number is not in the assembled context, it cannot appear in the commentary.

How it is built

  • Deterministic calculation layer. Variance, KPI movement and premium forecasts are computed in code, versioned, and reproducible on demand.
  • Governed context assembly. Only approved figures and their definitions are assembled into the prompt — not the raw table, and not free-text a user pasted in.
  • A hosted inference layer generates the draft narrative from that context and nothing else.
  • A human review boundary. The output is a draft. Nothing leaves the tool as management reporting without a person reading it, and the interface is built to make that step unavoidable rather than optional.
  • A live application — Streamlit over a cloud database — so the argument can be operated, not just described.

The connection to the research

The governance pattern behind this — separating deterministic computation from generative interpretation, and defining where a human has to sit — is the subject of the multi-agent architecture paper currently under review at Wiley's Intelligent Systems in Accounting, Finance & Management. The prototype is the argument in executable form.

Limits

It runs on synthetic, non-confidential data. It is a prototype demonstrating a control pattern, not a product, and it is published that way deliberately — a governed-AI claim that cannot be inspected is not worth making.

Background

I work where finance meets data engineering. That means SQL reporting workflows and finance data pipelines at the bottom of the stack, Power Query and a governed semantic model in the middle, and Power BI, Tableau and Streamlit applications at the top — with the research that argues for the pattern published alongside it.

The through-line is a single claim, which I have now argued in a working paper, a preprint, a journal submission, a conference talk, two university lectures and a trade feature: the spreadsheet is not the weak point. The pipeline feeding it is.

Alongside the practice I review for others — technical manuscripts for the publisher OrangeAVA, and submitted papers for the Global Business & Economics Journal. I am completing a PhD in Information Technology.
